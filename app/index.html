Home in Tacoma Explorer

What changed after Tacoma opened more residential land to more housing types?

Zoning reform effective February 1, 2025

Tacoma opened former single-family neighborhoods to more housing types.

Home in Tacoma replaced the former single-family zones with three Urban Residential districts: UR-1, UR-2, and UR-3. This explorer focuses on those three reform districts; Downtown and mixed-use centers follow different zoning rules.

Year One applications represented far more proposed homes, while the estimated number of distinct projects remained within the prior five-year range.

About this explorer

This independent portfolio project examines how housing applications changed after Tacoma's Home in Tacoma zoning reform took effect.

The application comparison is the main evidence. Zoning allowances and mapped environmental constraints provide context; they do not predict which parcels will be developed.

How the comparison works

Comparable 12-month periods

The first year under Home in Tacoma is February 2025–January 2026. It is compared with each of the five preceding 12-month periods and their average. Cancelled and voided applications are excluded.

Applications, projects, and units

“Applications” are building permit records. Related applications are grouped into “estimated projects” using project references, parcel, date, and housing type. “Proposed units” come from unit fields or clear application descriptions. Estimated projects are not official City project counts.

Urban Residential districts

The comparison covers UR-1, UR-2, and UR-3 because Home in Tacoma created these districts to replace former single-family zoning. Today's district boundaries are also used for the earlier applications because historical parcel-level boundaries were unavailable.

Environmental constraints

Parks, schools, rights-of-way, utilities, and other clear non-housing uses are excluded from the parcel map. Mapped steep slopes, wetlands, biodiversity areas, flood hazards, and protected-water buffers are combined to show where site review may be needed.

Important limitations

  • The comparison does not separate zoning effects from interest rates, construction costs, owner decisions, or project timing.
  • Today's Urban Residential boundaries are applied to earlier applications because historical parcel-level zoning was unavailable.
  • Permit descriptions, unit counts, project grouping, and parcel matching are imperfect. Results are checked against, but do not reproduce, the City's internal totals.
  • The parcel map excludes clear non-housing uses. The citywide comparison includes all non-cancelled housing applications in the three reform districts, so map totals can differ from the headline totals.
  • Applications are not the same as permits issued, construction starts, completed homes, or net new housing.
  • Housing allowed by zoning is a gross estimate and does not subtract existing homes.
  • Environmental maps are a first screen, not a field survey or site-specific determination. Public utility-easement boundaries were unavailable.
